# The Chain

“Open it,” the woman said, “but it has to stay locked.”

I looked at the brass cylinder, then at her. Behind us the care home's lunch trolley rattled into the lift. She had introduced herself as the daughter, which was what people said when they expected it to function as a key.

“Which is it?”

“He'll check.”

The door had a glazed panel above the handle. Through it I could see an old man asleep beside a radiator, his slippers planted neatly on yesterday's newspaper. A chain crossed the inside. His daughter had a key to the cylinder. The chain was the problem.

“I thought you meant he was locked in.”

“No. He locks us out.”

She showed me a plastic tub of soup. The lid had bowed with heat. In the corridor she had been holding it beneath her coat as though keeping something alive.

There was enough clearance to release the chain with a loop of wire. I could have had it done before the trolley returned. Instead I knocked with the plastic handle of my screwdriver.

The man woke and held up one finger. We waited. He found his glasses, folded the newspaper and walked to the door. He looked first at my tools, then at the tub.

“She has a key,” he said.

“Not to that.” I pointed at the chain.

He smiled for the first time.

His daughter asked whether I could remove it while I was there. I put the screwdriver away and offered to adjust the stiff latch instead. That needed his permission too.

He slid the chain free. When he took the soup, the steam had written a small white oval on the glass between them.
