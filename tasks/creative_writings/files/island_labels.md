## 1. Surveyor's chart, 1841

This chart records Bellwether as uninhabited. Its maker marked six freshwater springs but no dwellings. The brown rectangles near the eastern shore were catalogued as rock formations until 1978, when a conservator found small chimney strokes beneath a later wash of blue ink.

## 2. School bell, 1856

Recovered from a mainland warehouse, this bell bears the name of a school absent from official island accounts. A shipping receipt describes it as scrap. Wear inside the mouth suggests prolonged use, although the inscription alone cannot establish whether it ever rang on Bellwether.

## 3. Woman's boot, 1862

The boot was donated as an explorer's souvenir. Inside the sole, a repairer stitched a strip from a school exercise book. The surviving sentence reads, “When we return in spring.” The writer's name and the reason for leaving are missing from the folded paper.

## 4. Evacuation notice, 1863

Officials cited an approaching flood when ordering residents to leave. Earlier displays called this a successful rescue. The printer's ledger dates the notice three months before the first recorded storm warning. No passenger list survives; several families appear in mainland workhouse registers that winter.

## 5. Dredger's bucket tooth, 1864

Fishermen found this iron tooth where the western spring once rose. The harbour authority denied dredging beyond the shipping channel. Its stamped contractor's number also appears on invoices for “improvement of vacant ground”. Those invoices do not identify an island, depth, or final destination.

## 6. Glass photographic plate, 1901

At low water, three figures stand beside a straight line of stones. The donor called them tourists visiting a drowned village. A note on the envelope says, “Our front step.” We cannot name the figures. Their reflections occupy the space where the surveyor drew open sea.
