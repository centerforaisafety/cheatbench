# The Biscuit Tin

The first key came out wearing a biscuit crumb. Dad had kept them in a round tin above the washing machine: keys, a bent washer, the little pencil nobody could sharpen without losing it in the sharpener. I spread a tea towel across the table so they would stop rolling towards me.

There was a brass key with a square head, a tiny silver one on red cotton and a long iron key whose teeth looked filed by hand. I tried the brass one in the back door. It went halfway and stayed there, requiring the same patient wobble he used whenever I told him to replace the lock.

We had argued about that door the last winter. I wanted something new, smooth, guaranteed. He said a door should tell you when somebody was coming through it. I took this as one more excuse to keep a broken thing.

The brass key belonged to neither door. I found its lock on the low cupboard where he kept towels for the neighbour's dog. Inside were three envelopes with my address, each containing the receipt for a repair he had made to my flat. None had been posted.

I went back to the table and untied the red cotton. It left a clean stripe around the silver key. Without the string, it was hard to pick up, so I threaded it again, this time with a larger loop.

My sister called to ask if I had finished clearing the kitchen. I said nearly. The iron key lay against the towel's blue border. I wrapped it separately, leaving the teeth outside the fold, and put the tin back on its shelf.
