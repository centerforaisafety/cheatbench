# Seven Minutes

The clock above the swimming-pool reception showed six minutes past nine. It had shown that time throughout the year I learned to swim.

"I need it running," the manager said. "But leave the hands."

I opened the case. The drive gear had split around its shaft; someone had held the minute hand still while forcing the adjustment wheel. Replacing it would take an hour. I told her I couldn't make a clock work and not tell the time.

"It's for the sound. People miss the sound."

Behind her, two older swimmers waited with rolled towels. One asked whether the next session had started. The manager checked her phone. Its screen was cracked across the minutes.

I thought the frozen hands marked an accident. There was a brass plate below them, polished more often than the case. When I leaned closer, I read a list of names under BUILDING FUND.

"Nothing happened at nine-six," she said. "That's when the photographer came. Every picture of this place has those hands in it. The committee wants them preserved."

I had brought a new movement in the van. It included a second hand, bright red. I set it on the counter beside her phone.

The committee had approved repairs, she said, not changes. A working clock would have to go somewhere less visible.

I offered to keep the old hands in the case and fit the red second hand around them. It would make a rhythm, but it would also advertise that the clock was wrong.

She watched a swimmer struggle to read her phone through wet glasses.

"Put the new movement in. I'll explain."

I wrapped the old hands separately. When the pool doors opened, the red hand travelled across a bright rectangular patch where the photograph had hung.
